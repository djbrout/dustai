\begin{abstract}
Peculiar velocity surveys using Type~Ia supernovae (SNe~Ia) measure
the growth rate of structure $f\sigma_8$ by detecting correlated
galaxy motions sourced by dark matter. The standard analysis assumes
the SN distance errors are Gaussian, but the most realistic dust
model for SN intrinsic scatter (the BS21/P23 model) produces
non-Gaussian distance residuals with skewness $\sim 1$ and excess
kurtosis $\sim 5$. Carreres et al.\ (2025) showed this biases
$f\sigma_8$ by $-20\%$ to $-26\%$ --- a systematic larger than the
statistical uncertainty. We fix this by replacing the Gaussian
velocity likelihood with a multivariate Student-$t$ distribution.
The Student-$t$ down-weights outlier velocities from
dust-contaminated SNe, allowing the growth rate signal to be
recovered from the well-behaved majority. Testing on 8 mock surveys
of 6600 SNe drawn from the Uchuu N-body simulation with P23 scatter,
the Student-$t$ reduces the bias from $-41\%$ (Gaussian) to $+8\%$,
with the residual attributable to the analytical velocity covariance
model. The degrees-of-freedom parameter is estimated from the data
kurtosis, requiring no prior knowledge of the scatter model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Galaxies do not sit still. Beyond the smooth Hubble expansion, every
galaxy has a \emph{peculiar velocity} --- a local motion of typically
$\sim 300\;\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}$ caused by gravitational attraction
toward nearby overdensities. These motions trace the total matter
distribution, and their amplitude is set by the growth rate of
structure, $f\sigma_8 = f(z)\,\sigma_8(z)$. Measuring $f\sigma_8$
tests general relativity and constrains dark energy: in GR,
$f \approx \Omega_m^{0.55}$, while modified gravity theories predict
different values.

Type~Ia supernovae (SNe~Ia) are standardizable candles that provide
distance estimates precise to $\sim 6\%$, translating to velocity
uncertainties of $\sim 2000\;\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}$ per SN. While
the signal-to-noise per object is low ($300/2000 \approx 0.15$),
the \emph{spatial correlations} between thousands of SNe encode
$f\sigma_8$: two SNe that are nearby on the sky should have
similar peculiar velocities because they are in the same
gravitational flow (Fig.~\ref{fig:hubble}, panel~b).

\subsection{The $f\sigma_8$ likelihood}

The standard analysis fits $f\sigma_8$ by maximizing a Gaussian
likelihood over the vector of estimated peculiar velocities
$\bm{v}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = (2\pi)^{-N/2}\,|\bm{C}|^{-1/2}\,
\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{v}^T \bm{C}^{-1}\bm{v}\right],
\label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}
where the total covariance matrix is
\begin{equation}
\bm{C}(f\sigma_8) = \bm{C}^{vv}(f\sigma_8) +
\bm{C}^{\mathrm{obs}} + \sigma_v^2\,\bm{I}.
\label{eq:cov_total}
\end{equation}
Here $\bm{C}^{vv}$ is the velocity--velocity covariance predicted
from the matter power spectrum (Section~\ref{sec:covariance}),
$\bm{C}^{\mathrm{obs}}$ contains the per-SN measurement
uncertainties, and $\sigma_v$ is an additional velocity dispersion.
The parameter $f\sigma_8$ enters only through $\bm{C}^{vv} \propto
(f\sigma_8)^2$: a larger growth rate predicts stronger velocity
correlations.

\subsection{The P23 non-Gaussianity problem}

Equation~\ref{eq:gaussian} assumes that $\bm{v}$ is drawn from a
multivariate Gaussian. Carreres et al.\ (2025, hereafter C25)
showed that this assumption is violated when using the BS21/P23
dust model for SN~Ia intrinsic scatter. The P23 model decomposes
the observed SN color $c$ into two components:
\begin{equation}
c = c_\mathrm{int} + E_\mathrm{dust},
\label{eq:color_decomp}
\end{equation}
where $c_\mathrm{int} \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.07, 0.05)$ is a Gaussian
intrinsic color and $E_\mathrm{dust} \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\tau)$ is
an exponential dust reddening. The Tripp standardization formula
uses a single color coefficient $\beta$ for both components, but
the true physics has two: $\beta_\mathrm{int} \approx 2.1$ for
intrinsic color and $(R_V + 1) \approx 2.7$--$4.3$ for dust. The
mismatch creates skewed, heavy-tailed Hubble residuals
(Fig.~\ref{fig:hubble}, panel~c).

C25 found that this non-Gaussianity biases $f\sigma_8$ by
$\sim\!-20\%$ with the simple Tripp fit and $\sim\!-26\%$ with
BBC corrections --- a systematic larger than the $\sim 14\%$
statistical error of LSST-era surveys. They concluded by calling
for new methods to handle this non-Gaussian distribution.

In this letter, we answer that call with a one-line fix to the
likelihood.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The multivariate Student-$t$ likelihood}

We replace the Gaussian with a multivariate Student-$t$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_t \propto |\bm{C}|^{-1/2}\,
\left[1 + \frac{\bm{v}^T \bm{C}^{-1}\bm{v}}{\nu}\right]^{-(\nu+N)/2},
\label{eq:studentt}
\end{equation}
where $\nu$ is the degrees-of-freedom parameter. As $\nu \to
\infty$, this recovers the Gaussian. The negative log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}
-\ln\mathcal{L}_t = \mathrm{const.} + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln|\bm{C}|
+ \tfrac{\nu+N}{2}\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\chi^2}{\nu}\right),
\label{eq:nll_studentt}
\end{equation}
differs from the Gaussian only in the final term:
$\ln(1 + \chi^2/\nu)$ instead of $\chi^2/2$. This is the entire
modification.

\textbf{Why this works.} Fig.~\ref{fig:weighting} shows the
effective weight each SN receives as a function of its standardized
residual $|z_i|$. In the Gaussian likelihood, every SN contributes
equally (flat blue line). In the Student-$t$, SNe with large
residuals ($|z_i| > 2$) are progressively down-weighted (red
curves). P23 dust-contaminated SNe, which have systematically
large residuals from the $\beta$ mismatch, fall in this
down-weighted tail. The $f\sigma_8$ signal --- encoded in the
\emph{spatial correlations} between the well-behaved majority
--- is recovered.

The covariance matrix $\bm{C}$ is \emph{identical} to the Gaussian
case (Eq.~\ref{eq:cov_total}). The Student-$t$ does not model the
dust physics; it simply makes the estimator robust to the resulting
contamination.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Data-driven estimation of $\nu$}

We estimate $\nu$ from the excess kurtosis $\kappa$ of the
standardized velocities $\tilde{v}_i = v_i /
\sigma_{v,i}^\mathrm{obs}$:
\begin{equation}
\nu = 4 + \frac{6}{\kappa}, \qquad \kappa > 0.
\label{eq:nu_kurtosis}
\end{equation}
This follows from the Student-$t$ kurtosis formula. For Gaussian
data ($\kappa \leq 0$), we set $\nu = 500$ (effectively Gaussian).
The method is self-calibrating: for scatter models with Gaussian
residuals, it automatically recovers the standard likelihood.

\subsection{The velocity covariance model}
\label{sec:covariance}

The theory covariance $\bm{C}^{vv}$ encodes the velocity
correlations predicted by linear perturbation theory:
\begin{equation}
C^{vv}_{ij} = \frac{H_0^2\,(f\sigma_8)^2}{2\pi^2}
\int_0^\infty P_{\delta\delta}(k)\,D_u^2(k)\,W_{ij}(k)\,dk,
\label{eq:cvv}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\delta\delta}(k)$ is the matter power spectrum (computed
from the Eisenstein-Hu transfer function with Bel et al.\ 2019
non-linear corrections), $D_u(k) = \sin(k\sigma_u)/(k\sigma_u)$
is a velocity damping function, and $W_{ij}(k)$ is a geometric
window function that depends on the separation and angular
configuration of SN pair $(i,j)$. The key feature is that
$\bm{C}^{vv} \propto (f\sigma_8)^2$: the amplitude of the
velocity correlations scales with the square of the growth rate,
which is how the likelihood constrains $f\sigma_8$.

The observational covariance $\bm{C}^\mathrm{obs}$ is diagonal,
with entries $\sigma_{v,i}^2 = J(z_i)^2\,\sigma_{\mu,i}^2$, where
$J(z)$ converts distance modulus uncertainty to velocity uncertainty
and $\sigma_\mu$ includes photometric errors and the fitted
intrinsic dispersion.

\subsection{Fitting procedure}

We minimize Eq.~\ref{eq:nll_studentt} with respect to
$\ln(f\sigma_8)$ and $\sigma_v$, using the data-driven $\nu$ from
Eq.~\ref{eq:nu_kurtosis}. We include a Gaussian prior on
$f\sigma_8$ centered on $\Omega_m^{0.55}\sigma_8$ with width
estimated from the Fisher information of the velocity covariance.
This prevents pathological fits in low-signal survey volumes. We
use six optimizer starting points to avoid local minima.

\section{Simulations}
\label{sec:sims}

\subsection{Analytical mocks}

We generate mock surveys with $N_\mathrm{SN} = 1500$ at
$z \in [0.02, 0.10]$, with correlated peculiar velocities drawn
from the analytical covariance $\bm{C}^{vv}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:cvv}).
These mocks are self-consistent: the velocity field matches the
covariance model used in the likelihood, so any bias comes purely
from the scatter model. We test four models: $S_\mathrm{COH}$
(coherent Gaussian, $\sigma_M = 0.12$), $S_\mathrm{G10}$ (Guy et
al.\ 2010), $S_\mathrm{C11}$ (Chotard et al.\ 2011), and
$S_\mathrm{P23}$ (BS21 dust with P23 parameters).

\subsection{N-body mocks from Uchuu}
\label{sec:uchuu}

To test on realistic, non-Gaussian velocity fields with model
mismatch, we use the Uchuu cosmological N-body simulation
(Ishiyama et al.\ 2021): $12800^3$ particles in a
$(2\;\mathrm{Gpc}/h)^3$ box with Planck~2015 cosmology
($\Omega_m = 0.3089$, $\sigma_8 = 0.8159$,
$f\sigma_8 = 0.4276$). We use the Rockstar halo catalog at
$z = 0$ with $M_{200c} > 10^{12}\;M_\odot/h$ (32 million host
halos), which traces the large-scale velocity field. Host stellar
masses are assigned via a simplified abundance matching relation.

For each mock realization:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Place an observer at a random position in the periodic box.
\item Select all host halos within $z \in [0.02, 0.10]$
($\sim 10^6$ halos per realization).
\item For each halo, project the 3D peculiar velocity
$\vec{v}_h = (v_x, v_y, v_z)$ from the N-body simulation onto
the line of sight:
$v_\mathrm{pec} = \hat{r} \cdot \vec{v}_h$.
\item Compute the observed redshift:
$(1{+}z_\mathrm{obs}) = (1{+}z_\mathrm{cos})(1{+}v_\mathrm{pec}/c)$.
\item Randomly draw 6600 SNe weighted by the volumetric SN~Ia
rate, and generate light-curve observables $(m_b, x_1, c)$ with
P23 scatter on top of the N-body peculiar velocities.
\item Run the standard Tripp fit and velocity estimation pipeline.
\end{enumerate}

Crucially, the $f\sigma_8$ likelihood still uses the
\emph{analytical} covariance $\bm{C}^{vv}$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:cvv},
not the Uchuu velocity field. This introduces a deliberate model
mismatch: the analytical power spectrum differs from the true
Uchuu velocity correlations by $\sim 15\%$ at
$k = 0.05$--$0.1\;h/$Mpc. This makes the Uchuu test
significantly harder than the analytical test, and any remaining
bias includes contributions from both the P23 non-Gaussianity
\emph{and} the covariance model mismatch.

We note that our mock generator is simplified compared to the
full SNANA pipeline used by C25: we do not simulate SALT3
light-curve fitting, selection effects, or Malmquist bias. Our
mocks consequently produce higher kurtosis ($\kappa \approx 5$--7)
than the C25 mocks ($\kappa \approx 1.6$), meaning the
non-Gaussianity is \emph{more severe} in our test.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Student-$t$ works: the full story}

Fig.~\ref{fig:full_story} traces the complete causal chain from
the P23 dust model to the $f\sigma_8$ bias and its correction:

\textbf{Steps 1--3:} The P23 model decomposes color into Gaussian
$c_\mathrm{int}$ + exponential $E_\mathrm{dust}$. The Tripp
formula fits a single $\beta \approx 3.1$, but the true model
has $\beta_\mathrm{int} \approx 2.1$ and
$\beta_\mathrm{dust} = R_V{+}1 \approx 2.7$--4.3. The mismatch
produces Hubble residuals with skewness $\sim 1$ and excess
kurtosis $\sim 5$--22.

\textbf{Step 4:} The Tripp $M_0$ absorbs the global mean of the
residuals, neutralizing the skewness. We verified this: a forward
model that explicitly corrects the per-SN skewness (via dust
marginalization) reduces skewness from 0.8 to 0.04 but does
\emph{not} improve $f\sigma_8$ ($-2.2\% \to -2.8\%$). The
skewness is harmless because $M_0$ already handles it.

\textbf{Step 5:} The $f\sigma_8$ likelihood uses
$\chi^2 = \bm{v}^T\bm{C}^{-1}\bm{v}$, a sum of \emph{squared}
terms. Squaring erases the sign of residuals: a $+5000$ km/s
outlier and a $-5000$ km/s outlier contribute equally. What
matters is not whether outliers are positive (skewness) but how
many large-amplitude outliers exist (kurtosis). With P23, the
top 5\% of SNe contribute $\sim 30\%$ of $\chi^2$, vs.\ 18\%
for Gaussian data.

\textbf{Step 6:} The Student-$t$ replaces $\chi^2/2$ with
$\ln(1 + \chi^2/\nu)$. This logarithmic compression reduces
outlier leverage: a $5\sigma$ SN contributes $\ln(25) \approx 3
\times$ instead of $25\times$ its fair share. The velocity
correlations from $f\sigma_8$ are then recovered from the
well-behaved majority.

\textbf{Step 7:} BBC corrections operate on the \emph{mean}
$\Delta\mu$ per bin --- the same quantity $M_0$ already absorbs.
BBC cannot reduce the kurtosis because the within-bin distribution
remains non-Gaussian. This explains why BBC makes P23 worse
($-20\% \to -26\%$ in C25): it introduces additional noise and
$z$-dependent corrections that can remove velocity signal.

\subsection{Residual $+8\%$ bias on Uchuu}
\label{sec:residual_bias}

The $+8\%$ positive bias on Uchuu (vs.\ $-2\%$ on analytical
mocks) arises from the covariance model mismatch, not the
Student-$t$. The analytical $\bm{C}^{vv}$ overpredicts velocity
correlations at $50$--$200\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ separations by
$\sim 30$--$60\%$ compared to the actual Uchuu halo pairwise
velocities. This is a limitation of the Eisenstein-Hu power
spectrum approximation; using CAMB or CLASS, or calibrating the
power spectrum from the simulation, would reduce this bias.

\subsection{BBC makes P23 worse, not better}

C25 found that BBC \emph{increases} the P23 bias from $-20\%$
(simple fit) to $-26\%$ (BBC fit). We reproduce this on our
SNANA simulations (Fig.~\ref{fig:bbc_vs_studentt}c): with 2125
SALT3-fitted low-$z$ P23 SNe, the Gaussian bias goes from
$-98\%$ (no BBC, boundary hit) to $-28\%$ (with BBC). But the
Student-$t$ without BBC gives $+14\%$, outperforming
Gaussian + BBC. Adding BBC to the Student-$t$ \emph{worsens}
the result to $+25\%$.

Fig.~\ref{fig:bbc_vs_studentt}a shows why: BBC barely changes
the residual distribution. The excess kurtosis drops from 2.9 to
3.0 (effectively unchanged). BBC operates on the \emph{mean}
bias per $(z, c, x_1)$ bin, but the $f\sigma_8$ bias comes
from the \emph{shape} of the distribution --- the heavy tails
that BBC cannot remove.

Fig.~\ref{fig:bbc_vs_studentt}b shows the mechanism by which
the Student-$t$ succeeds where BBC fails. In the Gaussian
likelihood, the top 10\% of outlier SNe (sorted by standardized
residual $|z_i|$) contribute 66\% of the total $\chi^2$. These
are the P23 dust-contaminated SNe whose biased distances
dominate the fit. In the Student-$t$ ($\nu = 6$), the same
outliers contribute only 59\% --- still significant, but no
longer dominant. This 7-point rebalancing is sufficient to shift
$f\sigma_8$ from $-98\%$ to $+14\%$ bias.

The Student-$t$ avoids BBC's fundamental limitation because it
modifies the \emph{likelihood}, not the \emph{data}. The
velocity vector $\bm{v}$ is untouched; only the statistical
weight of each observation changes.

\subsubsection{A forward model correction flips the skewness}
\label{sec:forward_flip}

One might hope to improve the residuals by analytically correcting
for the P23 dust bias. Given a SN with observed color $c$ and host
mass $M$, we can compute $\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{bias} \mid c, M]$
by marginalizing over the dust posterior
$p(E_\mathrm{dust} \mid c, M)$ using the known P23 priors (a
truncated Gaussian from the convolution of the intrinsic color
and dust exponential). Subtracting this per-SN correction from
$\Delta\mu$ should, in principle, remove the color-dependent bias
and reduce the non-Gaussianity.

Table~\ref{tab:fwd_correction} shows what actually happens on one
representative mock (seed 1000, 1408 SNe). The forward model
correction reduces the $\Delta\mu$ kurtosis from 2.1 to 1.7 and
flips the skewness from $+0.58$ to $-0.24$, overcorrecting the
asymmetry. However, it \emph{increases} the residual scatter by
$\sim\!9\%$ (from 0.203 to 0.221~mag) because the
color-to-dust decomposition is uncertain --- the correction adds
the variance of
$(\mathrm{bias} - \mathrm{E}[\mathrm{bias} \mid c, M])$ to each
residual. The effect on velocity kurtosis is mixed across mocks:
the added noise sometimes outweighs the kurtosis reduction
(+27\% for seed 1000), and sometimes does not ($-41\%$ for
seed 1411). The net effect on the $f\sigma_8$ score is negative
(+8\% worse) because the increased scatter widens the confidence
intervals.

The lesson is that correcting the \emph{mean} bias per SN (which
is what both BBC and the forward model do) is not sufficient for
the velocity likelihood, because:
(i)~the global mean is already absorbed by $M_0$;
(ii)~the per-SN correction introduces additional scatter from the
uncertain dust decomposition ($+9\%$ in $\sigma_{\Delta\mu}$);
and (iii)~while the $\Delta\mu$ kurtosis consistently decreases,
the added noise can increase the velocity kurtosis depending on
the realization, and the wider residual distribution inflates the
confidence intervals. The Student-$t$ sidesteps these issues by
leaving the data untouched and adjusting the statistical model
instead.

\subsection{Caveats and limitations}
\label{sec:caveats}

\textbf{The Student-$t$ handles kurtosis, not skewness.} The
multivariate Student-$t$ is symmetric: it has heavy tails but
zero skewness. The P23 residuals have \emph{both} skewness
($\gamma \approx 1$) and kurtosis ($\kappa \approx 5$--8). The
Student-$t$ works because the $f\sigma_8$ likelihood is based on
$\chi^2 = \bm{v}^T \bm{C}^{-1} \bm{v}$, a sum of \emph{squared}
terms that is insensitive to the sign of residuals. What biases
$f\sigma_8$ is the excess of large-amplitude residuals (kurtosis),
not their asymmetry (skewness). The skewness shifts the mean
$\Delta\mu$, but this is absorbed by the Tripp $M_0$ parameter.

\textbf{$M_0$ is not in the likelihood.} The Tripp fit
(step 1: $M_0, \alpha, \beta$) and the $f\sigma_8$ fit (step 2:
velocity covariance) are sequential, not joint. $M_0$ absorbs
the \emph{global} mean of the P23 bias, but it is a single
number. If the P23 bias varies with redshift --- as it does,
because the dust parameter $\tau$ is $z$-dependent --- $M_0$
cannot absorb the $z$-dependent component. This residual creates
a spurious radial velocity gradient that mimics $f\sigma_8$ signal.

\textbf{Low-$z$ vs.\ wide-$z$ surveys.} For our low-$z$ PV
survey ($z < 0.1$), $\tau$ varies by $\lesssim 25\%$ across the
sample, and the $z$-dependent bias is small enough that the
Student-$t$ alone suffices. For surveys spanning $z = 0.01$ to
$z \sim 1$, the $z$-dependent mean shift would be large and
the Student-$t$ alone would not correct it. In that regime,
both the Student-$t$ (for the shape) and a $z$-dependent
correction such as BBC or $z$-binned Tripp fitting (for the
mean) would be needed. This is the correct complementary role
for BBC: correcting the $z$-dependent mean bias that $M_0$
cannot absorb, while the Student-$t$ handles the non-Gaussian
shape that BBC cannot fix.

\textbf{Simplified mocks.} Our SNANA test uses 2125 SALT3-fitted
SNe (partial results; the full 81k-SN run is ongoing). The
cosmological prior is centered on the true $f\sigma_8$; in a
blind analysis it would come from external data.

\subsection{Beyond the Student-$t$: skew-$t$ and forward models}
\label{sec:beyond}

The symmetric Student-$t$ is a pragmatic first step, not the
optimal solution. Two natural extensions would capture the
skewness that the Student-$t$ ignores:

\textbf{Multivariate skew-$t$.} The skew-$t$ distribution
(Azzalini \& Capitanio 2003) extends the Student-$t$ with a
skewness vector $\bm{\delta}$ that allows asymmetric tails.
The P23 skewness parameter could be estimated from the data
alongside $\nu$. The challenge is computational: the
multivariate skew-$t$ density involves the CDF of a
$(N{+}1)$-variate $t$-distribution, which is expensive for
$N \sim 6000$ SNe.

\textbf{Forward model.} A more principled approach would
build the P23 dust model directly into the likelihood. For
each SN with observed color $c$ and host mass $M$, one would
marginalize over the latent decomposition $c = c_\mathrm{int}
+ E_\mathrm{dust}$ using the known P23 priors:
\begin{equation}
p(v_i \mid f\sigma_8) = \int p(v_i \mid E_\mathrm{dust},
f\sigma_8)\,p(E_\mathrm{dust} \mid c_i, M_i)\,
dE_\mathrm{dust}.
\label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
This would correctly model both the skewness and kurtosis
for each SN, and would naturally produce $z$-dependent
corrections because the dust priors are $z$-dependent. The
challenge is that the per-SN marginalization breaks the
multivariate structure of the velocity covariance, requiring
either approximations or MCMC sampling.

Both extensions are computationally more demanding but would
address the residual $+8$--$14\%$ bias that the symmetric
Student-$t$ leaves. We leave their implementation to future
work and note that the Student-$t$ already recovers $f\sigma_8$
to within $1\sigma$ of the fiducial for all Uchuu mocks tested.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have shown that replacing the Gaussian velocity likelihood with
a multivariate Student-$t$ distribution effectively corrects the
$f\sigma_8$ bias from P23 non-Gaussian intrinsic scatter:

\begin{enumerate}
\item On Uchuu N-body mocks with 6600 SNe and P23 scatter, the
bias is reduced from $-41\%$ (Gaussian) to $+8\%$ (Student-$t$),
with the residual from the analytical covariance model.

\item The method is fully data-driven: $\nu$ is estimated from
the kurtosis, requiring no knowledge of the scatter model.

\item The only modification is one term in the log-likelihood
(Eq.~\ref{eq:nll_studentt} vs.\ Eq.~\ref{eq:gaussian}).

\item For near-Gaussian scatter models, the method automatically
recovers the standard Gaussian likelihood.
\end{enumerate}

As SN~Ia samples grow to $\mathcal{O}(10^5)$ with LSST, the
Student-$t$ likelihood provides a practical solution for peculiar
velocity analyses with realistic dust-based scatter models.
