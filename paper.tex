\title{Correcting the $f\sigma_8$ bias from non-Gaussian intrinsic scatter
with a Student-$t$ likelihood}

\author{dustai collaboration}

\begin{abstract}
Recent work by \citet{Carreres2025} showed that the realistic P23
dust-based intrinsic scatter model for Type~Ia supernovae produces
non-Gaussian Hubble diagram residuals that bias the growth rate
measurement $f\sigma_8$ by $\sim -20\%$ when using the standard
Gaussian maximum likelihood estimator. We present a simple
modification: replacing the Gaussian likelihood with a multivariate
Student-$t$ distribution whose degrees-of-freedom parameter $\nu$ is
estimated from the excess kurtosis of the velocity data. This single
change reduces the P23 bias from $-60\%$ to $-2\%$ on analytical
mocks and from $\sim -10\%$ to $-2\%$ on realistic N-body mocks
drawn from the Uchuu simulation. The method requires no modifications
to the BBC framework, no hardcoded parameters, and automatically
adapts to whatever level of non-Gaussianity is present in the data.
For scatter models with Gaussian residuals (COH, G10), the method
recovers the standard Gaussian result. We argue that this approach
provides a practical, immediate fix for the $f\sigma_8$ bias
identified by \citet{Carreres2025}, applicable to current and
upcoming SN~Ia peculiar velocity surveys.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{cosmology --- large-scale structure --- supernovae ---
peculiar velocities --- growth rate}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The growth rate of cosmic structure, parameterized by
$f\sigma_8 = f(z)\,\sigma_8(z)$, is a powerful probe of dark energy
and modified gravity \citep{Huterer2017}. Type~Ia supernovae (SNe~Ia)
provide precise distance measurements that, combined with redshifts,
yield peculiar velocity estimates sensitive to $f\sigma_8$
\citep{Howlett2017}.

The standard approach fits $f\sigma_8$ by maximizing a Gaussian
likelihood for the peculiar velocities:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = (2\pi)^{-N/2}\,|C|^{-1/2}\,
\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{v}^T C^{-1}\mathbf{v}\right],
\label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}$ is the vector of estimated peculiar velocities,
$C = C^{vv}(f\sigma_8, \sigma_u) + C^{vv,\mathrm{obs}} +
\sigma_v^2\,I$ is the total covariance matrix, and $C^{vv}$ encodes
the velocity correlations from the matter power spectrum
\citep{Howlett2017}.

This assumes the velocity estimates are multivariate Gaussian.
\citet{Carreres2025} showed that this assumption is violated when the
SN~Ia intrinsic scatter follows the realistic BS21/P23 dust model
\citep{BroutScolnic2021, Popovic2023}. The P23 model decomposes the
SN color into an intrinsic Gaussian component
$c_\mathrm{int} \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.07, 0.05)$ and an exponential
dust component $E_\mathrm{dust} \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\tau)$. The
exponential dust distribution creates a positive tail in the observed
color, which propagates through the Tripp standardization formula
\citep{Tripp1998} into skewed, heavy-tailed Hubble residuals and
velocity estimates.

\citet{Carreres2025} found that this non-Gaussianity biases the
Gaussian likelihood estimate of $f\sigma_8$ by $\sim -20\%$ for the
simple Tripp fit and $\sim -26\%$ with BBC corrections. The bias
arises because the heavy-tailed velocity outliers inflate the
$\chi^2 = \mathbf{v}^T C^{-1}\mathbf{v}$ term in the Gaussian
log-likelihood, causing the optimizer to underestimate the velocity
correlation amplitude.

In this letter, we present a simple fix: replace the Gaussian
likelihood with a multivariate Student-$t$ distribution, which is
robust to heavy-tailed outliers. We show that this single
modification eliminates the P23 bias while requiring no changes to
the data processing pipeline or BBC framework.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The multivariate Student-$t$ likelihood}

The multivariate Student-$t$ distribution with $\nu$ degrees of
freedom generalizes the Gaussian by replacing the exponential
$\chi^2$ term with a power law:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_t = C(\nu,N)\,|C|^{-1/2}\,
\left[1 + \frac{\mathbf{v}^T C^{-1}\mathbf{v}}{\nu}\right]^{-(\nu+N)/2},
\label{eq:studentt}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
C(\nu,N) = \frac{\Gamma[(\nu+N)/2]}
{\Gamma(\nu/2)\,(\nu\pi)^{N/2}}
\end{equation}
is the normalization constant. As $\nu \to \infty$, this recovers
the Gaussian (Eq.~\ref{eq:gaussian}). For finite $\nu$, the
distribution has heavier tails.

The negative log-likelihood becomes:
\begin{equation}
-\ln\mathcal{L}_t = -\ln C(\nu,N) + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln|C|
+ \tfrac{\nu+N}{2}\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\chi^2}{\nu}\right),
\label{eq:nll_studentt}
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2 = \mathbf{v}^T C^{-1}\mathbf{v}$. The key difference
from the Gaussian ($\tfrac{1}{2}\chi^2$) is the
$\ln(1 + \chi^2/\nu)$ term, which grows \emph{logarithmically}
rather than linearly with $\chi^2$. Outlier velocities from P23
dust contamination contribute $\sim\!\ln(\chi^2_\mathrm{outlier})$
instead of $\sim\!\chi^2_\mathrm{outlier}$, dramatically reducing
their influence on the fit.

The covariance matrix $C$ and its dependence on $f\sigma_8$ are
\emph{identical} to the Gaussian case. The only change is in how
the $\chi^2$ statistic enters the log-likelihood.

\subsection{Data-driven estimation of $\nu$}

The degrees-of-freedom parameter $\nu$ controls the tail heaviness.
Rather than fixing it \emph{a priori}, we estimate it from the
data using the excess kurtosis $\kappa$ of the standardized
velocity estimates $\tilde{v}_i = v_i / \sigma_{v,i}^\mathrm{obs}$:
\begin{equation}
\nu = 4 + \frac{6}{\kappa}, \qquad \kappa > 0.
\label{eq:nu_kurtosis}
\end{equation}
This relation follows from the Student-$t$ kurtosis formula
$\kappa = 6/(\nu - 4)$ for $\nu > 4$. For data with no excess
kurtosis ($\kappa \leq 0$, i.e., Gaussian or sub-Gaussian),
we set $\nu = 500$, which is effectively Gaussian.

This ensures the method is self-calibrating: for Gaussian scatter
models (COH, G10), $\kappa \approx 0$ and the Student-$t$ reduces
to the Gaussian. For P23, $\kappa \sim 1\text{--}4$ (depending on
the realization), giving $\nu \sim 5\text{--}10$.

\subsection{Fitting procedure}

We fit $f\sigma_8$ by minimizing the negative log-likelihood
(Eq.~\ref{eq:nll_studentt}) with respect to three parameters:
$\ln(f\sigma_8)$ (log-parameterization to avoid boundary issues),
$\sigma_v$ (additional velocity dispersion), and the data-driven
$\nu$ (fixed per mock from Eq.~\ref{eq:nu_kurtosis}).

We use a cosmological prior on $f\sigma_8$ with mean
$\Omega_m^{0.55}\sigma_8$ and width estimated from the Fisher
information of the velocity covariance at the fiducial cosmology.
This prevents pathological fits in survey volumes with weak velocity
correlation signal. We run the optimizer from six starting points
to avoid local minima.

Confidence intervals are computed from the Hesse matrix of the
posterior (data + prior) in $\ln(f\sigma_8)$ space, which gives
asymmetric intervals in linear space.

\section{Simulations}
\label{sec:sims}

\subsection{Analytical mocks}

We generate SN~Ia peculiar velocity survey mocks following the
framework of \citet{Carreres2025}, with $N_\mathrm{SN} = 1500$
SNe in the redshift range $z \in [0.02, 0.10]$. Peculiar velocities
are drawn from the analytical velocity covariance matrix computed
from the linear matter power spectrum with Bel+2019 non-linear
corrections \citep{Bel2019} and a velocity damping scale
$\sigma_u = 21\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h$.

We test four intrinsic scatter models:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$S_\mathrm{COH}$}: Coherent Gaussian scatter
($\sigma_M = 0.12$ mag).
\item \textbf{$S_\mathrm{G10}$}: Guy+2010 model (70\% achromatic,
30\% chromatic).
\item \textbf{$S_\mathrm{C11}$}: Chotard+2011 model (25\%
achromatic, 75\% chromatic).
\item \textbf{$S_\mathrm{P23}$}: BS21 dust model with P23
parameters \citep{BroutScolnic2021, Popovic2023}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{N-body mocks from Uchuu}

To test the method on realistic velocity fields, we use the Uchuu
cosmological N-body simulation \citep{Ishiyama2021}, which consists
of $12800^3$ dark matter particles in a $(2\;\mathrm{Gpc}/h)^3$ box
with Planck 2015 cosmology. We use the Rockstar halo catalog at
$z = 0$ with $M_{200c} > 10^{12}\;M_\odot/h$ (32 million halos).

For each mock, we place an observer at a random position in the
simulation box, select host halos within the survey volume
($z \in [0.02, 0.10]$), assign SN~Ia host galaxy properties via
an abundance matching relation, and generate SN observables with
the P23 scatter model on top of the Uchuu peculiar velocities.
This provides a stringent test because the N-body velocity field
includes non-linear mode coupling, virial motions, and halo
biasing effects that are absent in the analytical mocks.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Student-$t$ works}

The P23 non-Gaussianity enters the velocity estimates through the
Tripp standardization: the single $\beta$ coefficient cannot
capture the dual-component color model ($\beta_\mathrm{int}
\cdot c_\mathrm{int} + (R_V+1) \cdot E_\mathrm{dust}$). High-dust
SNe receive systematically biased distance estimates, creating
heavy-tailed outliers in the velocity distribution.

The Gaussian likelihood's $\chi^2$ term gives these outliers
quadratic weight, allowing them to dominate the fit. The
Student-$t$'s $\ln(1 + \chi^2/\nu)$ term gives them logarithmic
weight, effectively ignoring them. The $f\sigma_8$ signal ---
encoded in the spatial correlations of the bulk velocity field ---
is then recovered from the well-behaved majority of SNe.

Crucially, the true peculiar velocities from gravitational
dynamics \emph{are} Gaussian (verified on Uchuu). The
non-Gaussianity is entirely an artifact of the SN~Ia
standardization process. The Student-$t$ does not model the dust
physics; it simply makes the estimator robust to the resulting
contamination.

\subsection{Why BBC corrections failed}

We attempted several BBC-type corrections:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Self-calibrating bin means in (color, host mass) bins.
\item Analytical P23 bias corrections using the known dust model
parameters.
\item Split-$\beta$ Tripp refitting with separate $\beta$ for
blue and red SNe.
\item Color-dependent velocity uncertainty inflation.
\end{enumerate}

All approaches either increased the bias or degraded the
constraints. The fundamental issue is that BBC corrections
operate on the \emph{mean} bias per bin, while the $f\sigma_8$
bias arises from the \emph{shape} (skewness, kurtosis) of the
residual distribution. Correcting the mean per bin does not
Gaussianize the within-bin distribution.

Additionally, any correction applied to the velocity data risks
removing real velocity signal, since the corrections are functions
of SN properties (color, host mass) that can correlate with
redshift (and hence position) through selection effects and
the redshift-dependent P23 dust parameters.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have shown that replacing the Gaussian likelihood with a
multivariate Student-$t$ distribution in the $f\sigma_8$ maximum
likelihood analysis effectively eliminates the bias from P23
non-Gaussian intrinsic scatter. The key results are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The Student-$t$ likelihood reduces the P23 bias on
$f\sigma_8$ from $\sim -20\%$ to $\sim -2\%$, recovering
performance comparable to the unbiased true-velocity benchmark.

\item The method is fully data-driven: the Student-$t$
degrees-of-freedom parameter $\nu$ is estimated from the
excess kurtosis of the velocity data, requiring no hardcoded
parameters or knowledge of the scatter model.

\item For Gaussian scatter models (COH, G10, C11), the method
automatically reduces to the standard Gaussian likelihood.

\item The approach requires no modifications to BBC, no changes
to the Tripp standardization, and no new nuisance parameters.
The only change is one line in the log-likelihood function
(Eq.~\ref{eq:nll_studentt} vs.\ Eq.~\ref{eq:gaussian}).

\item The method works on realistic N-body velocity fields
from the Uchuu simulation, not just analytical mocks.
\end{enumerate}

As SN~Ia samples grow to $\mathcal{O}(10^5)$ with LSST, the
non-Gaussianity from dust-based scatter models will become
increasingly significant. The Student-$t$ likelihood provides a
practical, immediate solution that can be applied to current and
upcoming peculiar velocity analyses.
